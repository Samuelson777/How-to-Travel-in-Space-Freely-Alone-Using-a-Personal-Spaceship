## Abstract

This research paper explores the scientific, engineering, operational, legal, and safety requirements for enabling an individual human to travel in space alone using a personal spacecraft. The study examines whether “free personal space travel” is possible using current and near-future aerospace technologies. It discusses spacecraft architecture, propulsion, life-support systems, radiation protection, navigation, communication, autonomous control, emergency survival systems, power generation, thermal control, mission planning, and international space law.

The paper concludes that fully independent personal space travel is not currently practical for ordinary individuals because of high cost, launch dependency, life-support complexity, radiation risk, orbital regulation, and the need for continuous mission support. However, limited solo missions in low Earth orbit or controlled cislunar trajectories may become technically possible in the future if reusable launch systems, autonomous spacecraft, compact life-support systems, advanced radiation shielding, and legal frameworks continue to improve.

## Research Overview

The main purpose of this research is to study how a single person could theoretically travel in space using a personal spacecraft. The paper does not claim that unrestricted solo space travel is currently available. Instead, it presents a realistic technical pathway based on known physics, aerospace engineering, spacecraft systems, and existing human spaceflight practices.

The research focuses on the following core questions:

1. What systems are required for a one-person spacecraft?
2. Which propulsion technologies are realistic for personal space travel?
3. How can a solo astronaut survive in space without a large crew?
4. What role should automation and artificial intelligence play?
5. What safety systems are required for emergencies?
6. Can a personal spacecraft legally operate in orbit or deep space?
7. What future technologies are needed before personal space travel becomes practical?

## Major Topics Covered

### 1. Spacecraft Architecture

The paper proposes a compact spacecraft made of two major sections:

* **Pressurised crew module**
* **Service module**

The crew module would contain the astronaut, controls, life-support systems, emergency supplies, docking port, and radiation shelter. The service module would contain propulsion, propellant tanks, batteries, solar arrays, thermal-control systems, and avionics support.

### 2. Propulsion Systems

For near-term personal spacecraft, chemical propulsion remains the most practical option because it provides high thrust, restart capability, and emergency manoeuvring. Advanced propulsion concepts such as nuclear thermal propulsion, solar electric propulsion, fusion propulsion, and beamed propulsion are discussed as future possibilities, but they are not yet suitable for ordinary personal spacecraft use.

### 3. Life-Support System

A solo spacecraft requires reliable environmental control and life support. The paper discusses:

* Oxygen supply and monitoring
* Carbon dioxide removal
* Water storage and recovery
* Humidity control
* Waste management
* Fire detection and suppression
* Food storage and nutrition

For short missions, partial life-support regeneration is more realistic than a fully closed-loop system.

### 4. Radiation Protection

Space radiation is one of the greatest dangers for solo space travel. The paper proposes a compact storm shelter using onboard mass such as water bags, food packs, and equipment as shielding. The spacecraft must also include radiation monitoring and solar particle event warning systems.

### 5. Navigation and Communication

A personal spacecraft must use autonomous navigation supported by:

* Inertial measurement units
* Star trackers
* Sun sensors
* GNSS in Earth orbit
* Optical navigation
* Ground tracking support
* Near Space Network or Deep Space Network communication

The spacecraft should remain connected to mission control or emergency support networks, even if the astronaut is travelling alone.

### 6. Autonomous AI Systems

Because a solo astronaut cannot rely on a crew, the spacecraft must include strong autonomous systems. These systems should assist with navigation, fault detection, emergency response, health monitoring, docking, trajectory correction, and life-support management.

### 7. Legal and Ethical Considerations

Personal space travel cannot be completely unregulated. Any personal spacecraft would require national authorisation, launch and re-entry licensing, debris mitigation, insurance, tracking, and compliance with international space law.

Relevant legal frameworks include:

* Outer Space Treaty
* Liability Convention
* Registration Convention
* Rescue Agreement
* National launch and re-entry regulations

## Key Findings

The research identifies the following major findings:

* A personal spacecraft is technically possible only as a highly automated, heavily regulated, mission-supported vehicle.
* True “free travel” in space is limited by orbital mechanics, fuel, safety rules, life-support duration, radiation exposure, and legal control.
* Low Earth orbit is the most realistic first environment for personal space travel.
* Cislunar free-return missions may be possible in the future with reliable spacecraft and strong emergency systems.
* Deep-space personal travel is not practical with current technology for ordinary individuals.
* Autonomous control systems are essential for solo operation.
* Radiation shielding, rescue planning, docking ability, and mission control support are critical for survival.
* Personal space travel must follow international and national space regulations.

## Conclusion

Personal space travel using a one-person spacecraft is a scientifically interesting and technically possible future concept, but it is not yet practical as a fully free or independent activity. Space is not like air or sea travel because every mission requires precise orbital mechanics, high-energy launch systems, life-support reliability, radiation protection, thermal control, communications, emergency planning, and legal authorisation.

The most realistic near-term version of a personal spacecraft would not be a completely independent vehicle. It would be a compact, automated, mission-supported capsule launched by a certified launch provider, operated under national supervision, and connected to ground control. Such a spacecraft could potentially support short solo missions in low Earth orbit or carefully designed cislunar free-return missions.

For personal space travel to become more common, several technologies must improve: reusable launch systems, compact regenerative life support, autonomous spacecraft control, affordable propulsion, radiation shielding, orbital traffic management, emergency rescue systems, and international legal frameworks. Until these systems mature, personal spaceflight will remain limited to highly trained individuals, expensive commercial missions, and tightly controlled orbital operations.

This research concludes that “travelling freely alone in space” is not impossible, but it must be understood as a long-term engineering goal rather than a present-day personal transportation reality.
